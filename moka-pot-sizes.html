The Moka Pot "Cup" Is Not a Cup

How pot sizes, actual yield, and serving expectations cause confusion before the first brew.

A six-cup moka pot does not make six American cups of coffee. That single fact explains a remarkable amount of disappointment, overbuying, underbuying, and bad advice.

Moka-pot sizes are based on small Italian-style servings, not eight-ounce measuring cups and certainly not modern ten- or twelve-ounce mugs. A nominal "cup" is closer to a small concentrated serving. The exact output varies by manufacturer, model, how much water is loaded, how early the brew is stopped, and how much liquid remains below the funnel at the end.

What the numbers really mean

A one-cup pot is a tiny personal brewer. A three-cup pot generally produces one modest concentrated serving. A six-cup pot is often the practical choice for one person who likes a larger drink or for two people taking smaller servings. Nine- and twelve-cup pots are family or company pots, not merely slightly larger versions of the six.

The important number is not the label stamped on the box. It is the amount of brewed coffee you actually want in the cup.

Before buying a pot, decide whether you want

  • A small, intense straight serving.
  • A larger concentrated coffee diluted with hot water.
  • A milk drink built around moka coffee.
  • Two or more servings from one brew.

Then match the pot to that use. A pot that is too small cannot simply be "stretched" without producing a weak drink. A pot that is too large is not ideal for brewing half a basket. Moka pots are designed to be used with the basket filled evenly, so size should be chosen around the normal batch you intend to make.

Why yield is never exact

The water placed in the lower chamber is not the same as the liquid that reaches the upper chamber. Some water remains below. Some is retained by the coffee grounds. Some is lost as vapor. And if you stop the brew before the final harsh sputtering—as many careful brewers do—you deliberately leave a little more behind.

That is not wasted coffee. It is the price of stopping before the least pleasant part of the extraction enters the cup.

The practical way to choose

Ignore online arguments about whether a six-cup pot is "really" four cups or two cups. Measure your own drinking habits. Put the amount of finished moka coffee you enjoy into a measuring cup. If you add milk or water, measure the concentrated coffee before dilution. Then select a pot whose normal output is close to that amount.

It is also worth remembering that larger pots behave differently. They contain more metal, more water, and more coffee. They usually take longer to reach first flow and can have more momentum once the brew begins. A technique learned on a three-cup pot may need adjustment on a six- or nine-cup pot.

The best moka pot is not the one most enthusiasts call "standard." It is the one that produces the amount you actually drink, without forcing you to underfill it, over-dilute it, or brew a second batch immediately.

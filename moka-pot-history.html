History

The Little Pot That Changed Coffee

The moka pot brought strong, concentrated coffee into ordinary homes with a compact brewer that needed no pump, café machine, or special training.

Its octagonal form became one of the most recognizable objects in kitchen design. More important, it changed daily coffee habits by making a new kind of stovetop coffee convenient and affordable.

How it began

The moka pot's story starts with Alfonso Bialetti, an Italian metalworker who had spent years in France learning aluminum casting before returning home to Piedmont. In 1933, he introduced the Moka Express: an octagonal, aluminum stovetop pot that used steam pressure to push hot water up through ground coffee. It arrived during the Great Depression, when many Italians were visiting cafés less often, and it offered something new—café-style coffee made at home, on an ordinary stove, without any special equipment.

The eight-sided shape wasn't just decorative. The facets made the aluminum easier to cast and gave the pot a distinctive silhouette that made it instantly recognizable on a shelf—part of why it's remained largely unchanged for over ninety years.

From workshop to every kitchen

Alfonso's company stayed relatively small until his son, Renato Bialetti, took over after World War II. Renato is the one who turned the Moka Express from a well-made regional product into a household name: he modernized production, expanded distribution nationally, and built an advertising campaign around a cartoon figure with a moustache—modeled on Alfonso himself—that still appears on Bialetti pots today. By the postwar decades, the moka pot had become a fixture of Italian domestic life; it's often said that around nine in ten Italian households own one.

Two chambers, one funnel

The moka pot's mechanics are simple enough to see and hear working. Water goes in the lower chamber. A funnel-shaped metal basket, filled with ground coffee, sits on top of that lower chamber. The upper chamber then screws on over the funnel and collects the finished coffee. As the water in the bottom heats, steam pressure builds and forces the water up through the funnel, through the coffee grounds, and up a central spout into the top chamber—no pump, no electricity, just heat and physics.

How the design evolved

The core mechanism has barely changed since 1933, but the pot itself has: it now comes in sizes from a single-cup "mokina" up to family-sized pots holding a dozen or more cups, and in shapes ranging from the original octagon to rounded, cylindrical, and sculptural variants. Materials expanded too—classic polished aluminum is still the most common, but stainless-steel versions became popular for their durability and dishwasher tolerance, and more recently, induction-compatible pots (with a steel plate or steel construction) have let moka brewing move onto modern glass-top and induction stoves that plain aluminum can't heat directly.

What hasn't changed is the reason people keep buying it: it's mechanically simple, inexpensive to make and to own, easy to understand by watching it work, and it produces a cup that's genuinely different from both drip coffee and espresso. A design that solves a real problem well doesn't need to be reinvented—only adapted to new stovetops and new kitchens.

A design museum piece

The Moka Express's design has been recognized well beyond the kitchen. New York's Museum of Modern Art holds an example in its permanent design collection, crediting Alfonso Bialetti's 1933 design as one of the defining objects of 20th-century Italian industrial design—alongside its practical legacy as the pot that brought espresso-style coffee into the home.

Who makes moka pots today

Bialetti remains the name most associated with the moka pot, and along with the Colombian and Latin American cookware brand IMUSA, it represents the large-volume end of the market—mass-produced, widely distributed, and inexpensive. But it's far from the only maker worth knowing:

  • Alessi — the Italian design house known for collaborating with well-known architects and designers on high-concept, collectible moka pots.
  • Giannini — a long-running Tuscan manufacturer with a reputation among enthusiasts for build quality and classic proportions.
  • Ilsa — a Piedmont-based maker producing moka pots and other stovetop coffee equipment, often favored for its stainless-steel lines.
  • Pezzetti — a contemporary Italian brand known for colorful, design-forward pots aimed at a younger audience.
  • Cuisinox — a Canadian brand focused on all-stainless-steel, induction-ready pots for export markets.
  • Grosche — a Canadian company positioning its moka pots around ethical sourcing and sustainability.
  • IMUSA — a major Latin American cookware brand whose aluminum stovetop pots (often called cafeteras) are a household staple across much of Latin America and Latino communities elsewhere.

Broadly, Bialetti and IMUSA sit at the large-volume, mass-market end, while Alessi, Giannini, Ilsa, and Pezzetti represent the smaller, design- or craft-focused Italian tradition, and Cuisinox and Grosche represent newer, export-oriented manufacturers building specifically for stainless-steel and induction kitchens.
